# 18. KMR Representation of Zero and Subtraction: Deformation of Classical Arithmetic and Generation of New Equations

**Author**: Sergei Terikhov  
**Date**: 02.10.2026  

## Abstract

This paper investigates a property of KMR operators that allows the classical subtraction $t - 0$ to be represented as the difference of inverted KMR images. It is shown that in the KMR algebra, zero is not a neutral element in the classical sense: it is “remembered” as a nonzero quantity $1/A$, which cancels only upon subtraction. The parameter $A$ controls the degree of deformation, and the value $A = 1$ corresponds to degeneration into classical arithmetic. It is proposed to consider this as a one-parameter family of algebras and to pose the inverse problem: find $A$ such that a KMR expression coincides with a given classical identity. This opens a path to the generation of new equations.

## 18.1. Introduction

KMR operators (K-modulated Möbius reduction) are defined as fractional-linear transformations:

$$
A \odot K = \frac{A}{1 + KA}, \qquad A \oslash K = \frac{A}{1 - KA}.
$$

The formula reference `kmr_formulas_reference.md` records the basic properties of these operators: group structure, differential flow equations, scaling, and arithmetic representations. However, the existing materials do not address how KMR operators “see” zero and subtraction.

This paper fills that gap. We show that the classical identity $t - 0 = t$ admits a nontrivial KMR representation:

$$
t = (A \odot t)^{-1} - (A \odot 0)^{-1}.
$$

This means that in the KMR algebra, zero is represented by the quantity $1/A$, which does not cancel until subtraction. The parameter $A$ becomes a measure of the “memory” of zero.

## 18.2. Main Result

### 18.2.1. Identity for Subtraction of Zero

**Theorem 1.** For any $A \neq 0$ and any $t \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$
\boxed{t = (A \odot t)^{-1} - (A \odot 0)^{-1}}
$$

**Proof.** By the definition of the direct KMR operator:

$$
(A \odot t)^{-1} = \frac{1 + At}{A} = \frac{1}{A} + t,
$$

$$
(A \odot 0)^{-1} = \frac{1}{A}.
$$

Subtracting, we obtain $t$. $\square$

### 18.2.2. Role of the Parameter $A$

The identity can be rewritten as an affine deformation:

$$
K \mapsto \frac{1}{A} + K.
$$

For $A = 1$:

$$
(A \odot K)^{-1} = 1 + K,
$$

and the formula takes the classical form $t = (1 + t) - 1$. Thus, $A = 1$ is the point of “discrimination” of the KMR structure into classical arithmetic.

For $A \neq 1$, a family of algebras arises in which:

- the image of zero: $(A \odot 0)^{-1} = 1/A$;
- the image of $t$: $(A \odot t)^{-1} = 1/A + t$;
- subtraction: the difference of the images yields $t$.

### 18.2.3. Comparison with the Inverse Operator

An analogous identity holds for the inverse operator:

$$
(A \oslash t)^{-1} = \frac{1 - At}{A} = \frac{1}{A} - t.
$$

Then:

$$
(A \oslash 0)^{-1} - (A \oslash t)^{-1} = \frac{1}{A} - \left(\frac{1}{A} - t\right) = t.
$$

This gives a dual representation of subtraction via the inverse operator.

## 18.3. Interpretation

### 18.3.1. Zero as a “Hidden” Element

In classical arithmetic, zero is a neutral element: $t - 0 = t$. In the KMR representation, zero is “manifested” as $1/A$. This resembles situations in physics where the vacuum (zero state) possesses nonzero energy, or in algebra where zero can be represented by a nonzero operator.

### 18.3.2. Connection with q-Deformations

The affine deformation $K \mapsto 1/A + K$ is structurally close to q-deformations, where a deformation parameter controls the deviation from the classical case. However, unlike q-arithmetic, here the deformation is **additive** rather than multiplicative.

### 18.3.3. Inverse Problem: Finding $A$

A key consequence: if a classical identity is given, it can be rewritten in KMR form with an unknown $A$. Solving the equation for $A$ yields new algebraic relations.

**Example.** Suppose we need to find $A$ such that:

$$
\frac{A}{t + c} = (A \odot t)^{-1} - (A \odot c)^{-1}.
$$

The left-hand side is $\frac{A}{t+c}$. The right-hand side:

$$
(A \odot t)^{-1} - (A \odot c)^{-1} = \left(\frac{1}{A} + t\right) - \left(\frac{1}{A} + c\right) = t - c.
$$

The equation becomes:

$$
\frac{A}{t + c} = t - c \quad \Longrightarrow \quad A = (t - c)(t + c) = t^2 - c^2.
$$

Thus, the KMR representation of the fraction $\frac{A}{t+c}$ as a difference of images is possible only when $A = t^2 - c^2$. This is a new equation generated by the requirement that the KMR structure be consistent with the classical expression.

## 18.4. Generalizations

### 18.4.1. Arbitrary Subtraction

The identity easily generalizes to the difference of arbitrary arguments:

$$
t - c = (A \odot t)^{-1} - (A \odot c)^{-1}.
$$

This is a direct consequence of the affine deformation: $(A \odot K)^{-1} = 1/A + K$.

### 18.4.2. Composition

Using the group property $(A \odot K) \odot C = A \odot (K + C)$, one can build chains:

$$
t = (A \odot t)^{-1} - (A \odot 0)^{-1},
$$

$$
t + c = (A \odot (t + c))^{-1} - (A \odot 0)^{-1}.
$$

This allows sums to be represented via KMR chains.

### 18.4.3. Fractional-Linear Generalizations

Consider a more general form: $g(A,t) = (A^2 \odot t/A)^{-1}$. Then:

$$
g(A,t) = \frac{1}{A^2} + \frac{t}{A}.
$$

The difference:

$$
g(A,t) - g(A,c) = \frac{t - c}{A}.
$$

The inverse:

$$
\frac{A}{t - c} = (g(A,t) - g(A,c))^{-1}.
$$

This gives a KMR representation for fractions with a difference in the denominator.

## 18.5. Discussion

### 18.5.1. Why This Matters

Classical arithmetic “does not remember” zero. KMR operators make zero **observable** through the parameter $A$. This opens the possibility of constructing algebras in which the neutral elements of classical arithmetic become “active” and participate in equations.

### 18.5.2. Connection with Existing Sections of the Reference

The result is consistent with the “Arithmetic via KMR” section of the reference, where addition, multiplication, and division are expressed through KMR operators. The new identity adds subtraction of zero to this list and shows that zero occupies a special place among neutral elements.

### 18.5.3. Limitations

The identity holds for $A \neq 0$. For $A = 0$, KMR operators degenerate into the identity transformation, and the representation loses meaning. The domains of the operators must also be taken into account: $1 + At \neq 0$, $1 - Ac \neq 0$, etc.

## 18.6. Conclusion

We have shown that the classical subtraction $t - 0$ admits a nontrivial KMR representation:

$$
t = (A \odot t)^{-1} - (A \odot 0)^{-1}.
$$

This identity demonstrates that the KMR algebra “remembers” zero as the quantity $1/A$ and that the parameter $A$ controls the deformation of classical arithmetic. The inverse problem—finding $A$ from a given classical identity—generates new equations. The direction appears promising for further research: generalization to arbitrary neutral elements, connection with q-deformations, and applications in numerical methods and theoretical physics.
